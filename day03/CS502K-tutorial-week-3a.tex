\item
\question{Using the grammar of propositional logic from the bonus deck, show that the propositional formulae below are, or are not, syntactically correct.
\begin{itemize}
\item $P \lor Q \land S \Rightarrow S$
\item $P \Leftrightarrow Q \Rightarrow \lnot R$
\item $P \lnot (P \lor Q)$
\end{itemize}}
\answer{For each item, give a sequence of grammar rules which yields the formula.
\begin{itemize}
\item $P \lor Q \land S \Rightarrow S$ is correct:
\begin{align*}
\mi{Sentence} &\rightarrow \mi{ComplexSentence}\\
              &\rightarrow \mi{Sentence} \lor \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow \mi{AtomicSentence} \lor \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor \mi{ComplexSentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor \mi{Sentence} \land \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor \mi{AtomicSentence} \land \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor Q \land \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor Q \land \mi{ComplexSentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor Q \land \mi{Sentence} \Rightarrow \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor Q \land \mi{AtomicSentence} \Rightarrow \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor Q \land S \Rightarrow \mi{Sentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor Q \land S \Rightarrow \mi{AtomicSentence}\\
              &\rightarrow P \lor Q \land S \Rightarrow S
\end{align*}
\item $P \Leftrightarrow Q \Rightarrow \lnot R$ is correct; the sequence of grammar rules is similar to the previous item.
\item $P \lnot (P \lor Q)$ is not syntactically correct.
The grammar for propositional logic establishes $\lnot$ as a unary connective, so this formula is in fact
\[\mi{AtomicSentence}\,\lnot\,\mi{Sentence}\]
where $\mi{AtomicSentence}$ is $P$ and $\mi{Sentence}$ is $(P \lor Q)$. Using the grammar we obtain
\[\mi{AtomicSentence}\,\mi{ComplexSentence}\]
where $\mi{ComplexSentence}$ is $\lnot (P \lor Q)$. This can be further rewritten as
\[\mi{Sentence}\,\mi{Sentence}\]
The grammar does not allow two sentences to appear juxtaposed with no binary operator between them.
\end{itemize}}

write the meaning in English of the following formulae:

\item
\question{Prove via truth-tables whether the following formulae are valid (tautologies) or not:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(P \land \lnot P) \Rightarrow (\lnot Q \lor Q)$
\item $((P \land Q) \Rightarrow R) \Rightarrow ((P \lor Q) \Rightarrow R)$
\item $(P \land (Q \Rightarrow R)) \lor (Q \lor \lnot Q)$
\end{enumerate}}
\answer{Items (i) and (iii) are valid.
Item (ii) is not, because it fails on the two rows where exactly one of $P$ and $Q$ is true and $R$ is false.
Item (ii) is satisfiable rather than valid, so in the vocabulary of the bonus deck it is a contingency.

To build a truth-table, you should:
\begin{enumerate}
\item create a column for each proposition symbol;
\item create a column for each sub-formula, and one column for the complete formula;
\item create rows for all possible combinations of true and false values of the proposition symbols;
\item using the truth values of the proposition symbols, work out the truth value of each sub-formula, and use those values to work out the truth value of the complete formula.
\end{enumerate}
The meaning (truth value) of each logical operator is as follows:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|ccccc}
$\alpha_1$ & $\alpha_2$ & $\lnot \alpha_1$ & $\alpha_1 \land \alpha_2$ & $\alpha_1 \lor \alpha_2$ & $\alpha_1 \Rightarrow \alpha_2$ & $\alpha_1 \Leftrightarrow \alpha_2$\\
\hline
false & false & true  & false & false & true  & true\\
false & true  & true  & false & true  & true  & false\\
true  & false & false & false & true  & false & false\\
true  & true  & false & true  & true  & true  & true
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Our specific items have the following tables.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(P \land \lnot P) \Rightarrow (\lnot Q \lor Q)$
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|ccccc}
$P$ & $Q$ & $\lnot P$ & $(P \land \lnot P)$ & $\lnot Q$ & $(\lnot Q \lor Q)$ & $(P \land \lnot P) \Rightarrow (\lnot Q \lor Q)$\\
\hline
true  & true  & false & false & false & true & true\\
false & true  & true  & false & false & true & true\\
true  & false & false & false & true  & true & true\\
false & false & true  & false & true  & true & true
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The antecedent $(P \land \lnot P)$ is false in every model, so the implication is true in every model.

\item $((P \land Q) \Rightarrow R) \Rightarrow ((P \lor Q) \Rightarrow R)$
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc|cccc|c}
$P$ & $Q$ & $R$ & $(P \land Q)$ & $(P \lor Q)$ & $((P \land Q) \Rightarrow R)$ & $((P \lor Q) \Rightarrow R)$ & whole\\
\hline
true  & true  & true  & true  & true  & true  & true  & true\\
false & true  & true  & false & true  & true  & true  & true\\
true  & false & true  & false & true  & true  & true  & true\\
false & false & true  & false & false & true  & true  & true\\
true  & true  & false & true  & true  & false & false & true\\
false & true  & false & false & true  & true  & false & \textbf{false}\\
true  & false & false & false & true  & true  & false & \textbf{false}\\
false & false & false & false & false & true  & true  & true
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Two rows make the formula false, so it is not valid. It is satisfiable, since six rows make it true.

\item $(P \land (Q \Rightarrow R)) \lor (Q \lor \lnot Q)$ is a tautology.
The right disjunct $(Q \lor \lnot Q)$ is true in every model, so the whole disjunction is true in every model and no full table is needed.
\end{enumerate}}

\item
\question{Using the algorithm to enumerate all models studied in our lectures:

\medskip
\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $KB = \alpha_1 \land \cdots \land \alpha_n$, and $\alpha$ (a query)
\Ensure true or false
\Function{TT-Entails}{$KB, \alpha$}
  \State $\mi{symbols} \gets \mi{prop}(KB) \cup \mi{prop}(\alpha)$ \Comment{get symbols of $KB$ and $\alpha$}
  \State \Return \Call{TT-Check-All}{$KB, \alpha, \mi{symbols}, \emptyset$}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{minipage}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $KB$, $\alpha$, a set $\mi{symbols}$, and a partial $\mi{model}$
\Ensure true or false
\Function{TT-Check-All}{$KB, \alpha, \mi{symbols}, \mi{model}$}
  \If{$\mi{symbols} = \emptyset$}
    \If{$I(\mi{model}, KB) = \mi{true}$}
      \State \Return $I(\mi{model}, \alpha)$
    \Else
      \State \Return $\mi{true}$ \Comment{if $KB$ is false always return true}
    \EndIf
  \Else
    \State $\mi{rest} \gets \mi{symbols} \setminus \{P\}$ \Comment{pick one symbol $P$}
    \State \Return \Call{TT-Check-All}{$KB, \alpha, \mi{rest}, \mi{model} \cup \{(P, \mi{true})\}$}
    \State \qquad \textbf{and} \Call{TT-Check-All}{$KB, \alpha, \mi{rest}, \mi{model} \cup \{(P, \mi{false})\}$}
  \EndIf
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\medskip

\noindent show how the algorithm computes $\textsc{TT-Entails}(((P \land \lnot P) \land \lnot Q), Q)$.
Lecture 5 writes the test on line 4 as $\textsc{PL-True?}(\alpha, \mi{model})$; it computes the same thing as $I(\mi{model}, \alpha)$, which the bonus deck defines.}
\answer{Your answer should say how each variable in the algorithm was instantiated and how the instructions changed it.
The original invocation $\textsc{TT-Entails}(((P \land \lnot P) \land \lnot Q), Q)$ makes $KB = (P \land \lnot P) \land \lnot Q$ and $\alpha = Q$; line 2 computes $\mi{symbols} = \{P, Q\}$, and line 3 invokes $\textsc{TT-Check-All}((P \land \lnot P) \land \lnot Q,\ Q,\ \{P, Q\},\ \emptyset)$.

The definition of \textsc{TT-Check-All} then gives the final result, which is true.
The recursion reaches four leaves, one per model of $\{P, Q\}$, and $KB$ is false in every one of them because $KB$ contains the contradiction $P \land \lnot P$.
Every leaf therefore returns true by the ``if $KB$ is false always return true'' branch, so the conjunction of all four is true.
Entailment here holds vacuously: an inconsistent $KB$ entails everything, including both $Q$ and $\lnot Q$.

When you follow an algorithm by hand, keep track of the type of each variable, whether it is a set, an integer or a formula, and of how each operation changes it.
Recursive definitions repay particular care.}

\question{Answer the following items:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A propositional formula is a \emph{contradiction} if it is false in every possible model.
If $\lnot \alpha$ is a contradiction, what can we say about $\alpha$? Justify your answer.
\item If $\alpha$ is not a contradiction, what can we say about $\lnot \alpha$? Justify your answer.
\item How are truth tables related to the interpretation function $I(m, \alpha)$?
\item The bonus deck states that $KB \models \alpha$ if and only if $(KB \land \lnot \alpha)$ is unsatisfiable.
Justify that equivalence from the definition of entailment.
\end{enumerate}}
\answer{
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\lnot \alpha$ is a contradiction then $I(m, \lnot \alpha) = \mi{false}$ for every model $m$.
Dropping the negation flips every one of those to true, that is $I(m, \alpha) = \mi{true}$ for every model $m$, so $\alpha$ is valid, a tautology.
\item If $\alpha$ is not a contradiction then $I(m, \alpha)$ can be true or false depending on $m$.
We call such formulae \emph{contingencies}, and their truth values depend on the values the model gives the propositions.
If $I(m, \alpha)$ can be true or false depending on $m$, then $I(m, \lnot \alpha)$ can be false or true depending on $m$, so $\lnot \alpha$ is also a contingency.
The premise here is weaker than in the previous item, since ``not a contradiction'' says less than ``is a tautology'', and it buys a correspondingly weaker conclusion.
\item Each row of a truth table corresponds to one model $m$, and each cell in that row is the value of $I(m, \cdot)$ on the sub-formula heading its column.
\item $KB \models \alpha$ means that $\alpha$ is true in every model of $KB$, so no model makes $KB$ true and $\alpha$ false.
A model makes $(KB \land \lnot \alpha)$ true exactly when it makes $KB$ true and $\alpha$ false, so there is no such model, and having no model is exactly unsatisfiability.
Reading the argument in the other direction gives the converse.
Resolution rests on this equivalence.
\end{enumerate}}

\item
\question{Given the model $m = \{(P, \mi{false}), (Q, \mi{false}), (R, \mi{true})\}$ over the propositions $\{P, Q, R\}$, compute $I(m, ((P \Rightarrow \lnot Q) \land R))$.}
\answer{$I(m, ((P \Rightarrow \lnot Q) \land R)) = \mi{true}$ if and only if $I(m, (P \Rightarrow \lnot Q)) = \mi{true}$ and $I(m, R) = \mi{true}$.
Since $(R, \mi{true}) \in m$ we have $I(m, R) = \mi{true}$.
Now $I(m, (P \Rightarrow \lnot Q)) = \mi{false}$ only if $I(m, P) = \mi{true}$ and $I(m, \lnot Q) = \mi{false}$.
But $I(m, P) = \mi{false}$, because $(P, \mi{false}) \in m$.
So $I(m, (P \Rightarrow \lnot Q)) = \mi{true}$, and therefore $I(m, ((P \Rightarrow \lnot Q) \land R)) = \mi{true}$.}

\end{enumerate}

\question{Lecture 5 wrote one situation in the Wumpus world as five propositional sentences.
Let $P_{i,j}$ be true when square $[i,j]$ holds a pit, and $B_{i,j}$ be true when square $[i,j]$ is breezy.
After the agent has visited $[1,1]$ and $[2,1]$, feeling no breeze in the first and a breeze in the second, its knowledge base is $KB = R_1 \land R_2 \land R_3 \land R_4 \land R_5$, where
\begin{align*}
R_1&: \lnot P_{1,1} & R_2&: \lnot B_{1,1} & R_3&: B_{2,1}\\
R_4&: B_{1,1} \Leftrightarrow (P_{1,2} \lor P_{2,1}) & R_5&: B_{2,1} \Leftrightarrow (P_{1,1} \lor P_{2,2} \lor P_{3,1}) &&
\end{align*}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The full truth table over the seven propositions appearing in $KB$ has $128$ rows, and the lecture works with eight.
Which four propositions does $KB$ pin to a single value, and why does that leave eight rows?
\item Write out those eight models, over $P_{1,2}$, $P_{2,2}$ and $P_{3,1}$, and say which of them satisfy $KB$.
\item Using your answer, decide whether $KB \models \lnot P_{1,2}$ and whether $KB \models \lnot P_{2,2}$.
What does each verdict tell the agent to do next?
\end{enumerate}}
\answer{
\begin{enumerate}
\item $R_1$ gives $P_{1,1} = \mi{false}$, $R_2$ gives $B_{1,1} = \mi{false}$ and $R_3$ gives $B_{2,1} = \mi{true}$.
With $B_{1,1}$ false, the biconditional $R_4$ forces $P_{1,2} \lor P_{2,1}$ to be false, so $P_{2,1} = \mi{false}$ as well, and so does $P_{1,2}$.
Four of the seven propositions are therefore fixed, and any row disagreeing with them falsifies $KB$.
The three that remain free are $P_{1,2}$, $P_{2,2}$ and $P_{3,1}$, giving $2^{3} = 8$ rows worth writing down.

\item With $B_{2,1}$ true and $P_{1,1}$ false, $R_5$ reduces to $P_{2,2} \lor P_{3,1}$.
Writing the eight models as triples $(P_{1,2}, P_{2,2}, P_{3,1})$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|l}
$(P_{1,2}, P_{2,2}, P_{3,1})$ & satisfies $KB$? & why not\\
\hline
(false, false, false) & no  & $R_5$ needs $P_{2,2} \lor P_{3,1}$\\
(false, false, true)  & \textbf{yes} & \\
(false, true,  false) & \textbf{yes} & \\
(false, true,  true)  & \textbf{yes} & \\
(true,  false, false) & no  & $R_4$ forces $P_{1,2}$ false, and $R_5$ fails too\\
(true,  false, true)  & no  & $R_4$\\
(true,  true,  false) & no  & $R_4$\\
(true,  true,  true)  & no  & $R_4$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Three models satisfy $KB$, which matches the three highlighted rows of the lecture's $128$-row table.

\item $P_{1,2}$ is false in all three models of $KB$, so $KB \models \lnot P_{1,2}$ and the agent can step into $[1,2]$ knowing there is no pit.
$P_{2,2}$ is true in two of the three and false in the third, so neither $KB \models \lnot P_{2,2}$ nor $KB \models P_{2,2}$ holds, and $[2,2]$ stays unknown.
Entailment gives the agent a safe move and, just as usefully, refuses to give it an unsafe one.
\end{enumerate}}

\item
\question{Define a procedure, in pseudo-code, to convert a propositional formula into an equivalent formula in conjunctive normal form.
Your procedure should handle any formula built from the logical operators studied in our lectures, namely $\lnot$, $\lor$, $\land$, $\Rightarrow$ and $\Leftrightarrow$.}
\answer{This question is open-ended.
A solution should handle one operator at a time and use the existing functionality to process the resulting equivalent formula recursively.
The usual shape is four passes over the formula:
\begin{enumerate}
\item eliminate $\Leftrightarrow$, rewriting $\alpha \Leftrightarrow \beta$ as $(\alpha \Rightarrow \beta) \land (\beta \Rightarrow \alpha)$;
\item eliminate $\Rightarrow$, rewriting $\alpha \Rightarrow \beta$ as $\lnot \alpha \lor \beta$;
\item push $\lnot$ inwards using De Morgan's laws and double-negation elimination, until every negation sits on a proposition symbol;
\item distribute $\lor$ over $\land$, rewriting $\alpha \lor (\beta \land \gamma)$ as $(\alpha \lor \beta) \land (\alpha \lor \gamma)$, until the formula is a conjunction of disjunctions of literals.
\end{enumerate}
The order of the passes matters, because distributing before the negations are pushed in leaves negated conjunctions in the way.
The result can also be exponentially larger than the input, since step 4 duplicates $\alpha$ every time it fires.}

\item
\question{Let $KB = (A \Leftrightarrow (B \lor C)) \land \lnot B \land \lnot C$ and let $\alpha = \lnot A$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Convert $KB \land \lnot \alpha$ to conjunctive normal form, using the four passes of the previous question, and list the clauses.
\item Give a resolution refutation, stating at each step which two clauses you resolve and on which literal.
\item Suppose $\lnot C$ were dropped from $KB$.
Show that no refutation exists, by giving a model of the remaining clauses.
\end{enumerate}}
\answer{
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\lnot \alpha$ is $A$.
The biconditional $A \Leftrightarrow (B \lor C)$ becomes $(A \Rightarrow (B \lor C)) \land ((B \lor C) \Rightarrow A)$, then $(\lnot A \lor B \lor C) \land (\lnot (B \lor C) \lor A)$, and pushing the negation inwards turns the second conjunct into $(\lnot B \land \lnot C) \lor A$, which distributes to $(\lnot B \lor A) \land (\lnot C \lor A)$.
The five clauses are
\[C_1: \lnot A \lor B \lor C \qquad C_2: \lnot B \lor A \qquad C_3: \lnot C \lor A \qquad C_4: \lnot B \qquad C_5: \lnot C \qquad C_6: A\]
where $C_6$ is the negated query.

\item Resolve $C_6$ with $C_1$ on $A$, giving $C_7: B \lor C$.
Resolve $C_7$ with $C_4$ on $B$, giving $C_8: C$.
Resolve $C_8$ with $C_5$ on $C$, giving the empty clause.
So $KB \models \lnot A$.

\item Without $C_5$ the derivation stalls at $C_8: C$, and nothing resolves with it.
The model $\{(A, \mi{true}), (B, \mi{false}), (C, \mi{true})\}$ satisfies $C_1$ to $C_4$ and $C_6$, so the clause set is satisfiable and no refutation can exist.
Read back through the biconditional, this is the agent saying that $A$ holds because $C$ does, which is exactly what the removed sentence ruled out.
\end{enumerate}}

\item
\question{Take the Horn knowledge base
\begin{align*}
r_1&: W \Rightarrow Y  & r_2&: X \land Y \Rightarrow Z & r_3&: Y \Rightarrow V\\
r_4&: V \land Z \Rightarrow U & r_5&: X \Rightarrow T   & &\text{together with the facts } W \text{ and } X
\end{align*}
and the query $U$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Run forward chaining. Give the order in which propositions are added to the set of known facts.
\item Run backward chaining on $U$. Draw the tree of subgoals, and give the order in which they are proved.
\item One rule fires under forward chaining and is never touched by backward chaining.
Which one, and what does the difference say about when each algorithm is the one to reach for?
\end{enumerate}}
\answer{
\begin{enumerate}
\item The facts $W$ and $X$ start the agenda.
$W$ satisfies $r_1$, adding $Y$.
$Y$ satisfies $r_3$, adding $V$, and together with $X$ it satisfies $r_2$, adding $Z$.
$X$ alone satisfies $r_5$, adding $T$.
With $V$ and $Z$ both known, $r_4$ fires and adds $U$.
One admissible order is $W, X, Y, V, Z, T, U$; any order that respects those dependencies is correct, since forward chaining derives every consequence of the knowledge base whatever order the agenda imposes.

\item Backward chaining starts from $U$ and works down:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l@{}}
$U$ \quad by $r_4$, subgoals $V$ and $Z$\\
\quad $V$ \quad by $r_3$, subgoal $Y$\\
\quad\quad $Y$ \quad by $r_1$, subgoal $W$\\
\quad\quad\quad $W$ \quad a fact, succeeds\\
\quad $Z$ \quad by $r_2$, subgoals $X$ and $Y$\\
\quad\quad $X$ \quad a fact, succeeds\\
\quad\quad $Y$ \quad already proved\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The proofs complete in the order $W, Y, V, X, Z, U$.

\item Rule $r_5$ fires under forward chaining, adding $T$, and backward chaining never considers it, because $T$ appears in no subgoal on the way to $U$.
Forward chaining is data-driven and derives everything the knowledge base supports, which suits a fixed set of facts queried many times.
Backward chaining is goal-driven and touches only what the query needs, which suits a large knowledge base queried about one thing.
The cost is the mirror image: forward chaining pays for conclusions nobody asked for, and backward chaining repeats work across separate queries unless it caches.
\end{enumerate}}
